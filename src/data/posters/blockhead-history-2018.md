## Abstract

In the last half century, spindle accuracy specifications have gone from tenths to microns to microinches to nanometers and this is the story of how Professional Instruments helped get there.

In the pursuit of precision, the Blockhead air bearing spindle is the gold standard for accuracy and has been the workhorse at Professional Instruments since its invention. “The Blockhead has seen application in a huge variety of fields from basic physics research in the value of the gravitational constant to the fabrication of intraocular lenses for cataract surgery.” [1]

The history of the Blockhead and how it came to be requires an understanding of the history of Professional Instruments and how two brothers played a key role in the field of precision engineering.

## Introduction

Professional Instruments Company (PICo) was established in 1947, originally as a job shop, by Harold and Ted Arneson (Fig 1), and became known in the precision engineering world in 1960 with their groundbreaking work for Honeywell, in fabricating hollow spherical gyroscope rotors.

<blockquote class="pq"><p>“If it goes around and has to be good, PICo probably had something to do with it.”</p><p class="pq-by">- Ted Arneson</p></blockquote>

To meet Honeywell’s specifications, PICo needed an accurate spindle. This led Harold to invent the dual-hemisphere “Zeron” air bearing spindle. An interesting problem in measuring spindle accuracy is the need to separate out-of-roundness of the test ball from radial error motion of the spindle. As a result, the development of precision spheres coincided with the development of precision spindles. Having developed his own sphere lapping methods [2] for Honeywell’s gyroscopes, Harold inadvertently constructed an air bearing pivot which inspired the step bearing concept used for the Zeron spindle.

It did not take long before the Zeron spindle could no longer be improved, leading Harold and Ted to a different design, the Blockhead. The Blockhead spindle was produced and patented in 1969 and it became the most widely used ultra precision air bearing spindle in the world [1].

## A job shop

PICo started in a rented walk-up with basic tools and a desire to succeed (Fig 3). Harold (left) is shown here watching the assembly of a Zeromax stepless transmission – which became an education in precision grinding, metrology, and metallurgy.

<figure class="pf pf-narrow"><img src="/images/posters/blockhead-history-2018/fig3.webp" width="1200" height="1307" alt="Black and white photo of two men at a workbench in a small shop" loading="lazy" /><figcaption><span class="fn">Figure 3.</span> Harold (left) in PICo’s first shop, located in Minneapolis, Minnesota.</figcaption></figure>

The invention was intended to be made with just a punch press and a centerless grinder but it wasn’t that easy. In fact, it turned out to require a high level of skill and between-centers grinding which Harold mastered through careful study of center-lapping, spherical centers, balanced-force drivers, tailstock issues, wheel dressing and balancing, and quirks of the grinder itself.

In the 1950’s, Honeywell was in the need of suppliers for difficult-to-make gyroscope parts. They liked what they saw in Ted and Harold and fed them work of increasing difficulty. As a result, PICo acquired indicating mikes, diamond-tipped Mikrokators, and other equipment for working in the sub-tenth world (less than 0.0001 inch).

## Bearings for a perfect gyro

Professor Arnold Nordsieck (University of Illinois) (Fig 4) had conceived of an electrostatically suspended hollow-ball gyroscope that would allow submarines to remain submerged longer. Honeywell had taken up the work but needed someone to make the gyroscopes. Ted impressed Dr. Nordsieck with PICo’s potential and Harold began work on the job of making hollow beryllium balls to an unprecedented level of roundness; lapped to a few microinches, balanced to a 220-second pendulous period, and spun in a vacuum at 12,000 rpm. Due to the high accuracy required in this work it became necessary to develop a spindle with sub-microinch accuracy. Early efforts took the form of several types of oil lubricated bearings, but it soon became apparent that the spindle needed to be externally pressurized to maintain a fixed position while running or stopped. Harold’s answer to this was the Zeron dual-hemisphere air bearing spindle [5]. However, the Zeron’s shaft had to be long to reduce the influence from the rear bearing which limited its stiffness. The resultant reaction on the rear bearing, when a force was applied to the front bearing, caused too much flex. Although it was “kinematically correct”, the Zeron was not a practical spindle. It was good for measuring, not machining.

## Tapers - flutes

While working on gyros, Harold used an air purge to keep coolant out of a pivot bearing that had a honed, barrel-shaped hole. Surprised when it spun freely, he read up on early air bearings in Dudley Fuller’s book “Theory and Practice of Lubrication for Engineers” leading to Harold’s invention of the groove compensated air bearing. A groove compensated bearing has grooves which allow the air film to respond to applied loads with instant compensating changes in the bearing’s air flow to restore the bearing to a centralized condition [5].

## The Blockhead spindle

The Blockhead was born out of Ted’s desire to have an air bearing spindle that could be used on a grinder or lathe and also as a highly accurate roundness tester. The shaft carrying the radial load became short and stiff leaving axial and tilt properties to the large-diameter thrust plates. The net result is volumetric efficiency; a whole lot of load capacity in a compact package. The first 10B Blockhead sold, S/N 2, was purchased in 1967 by Goddard Space Flight Center. We kept S/N 1 and it is still being used to grind parts in our Heald 273A Internal Grinder.

Highly accurate spindles led to the development of a spherical pilot technology which allowed diamond wheels to be trued and dressed on one machine then transferred to another machine and still run true. This same technology was used for creating a gage—using a 4B Blockhead at its heart—to establish the location points for the instrument packages in the Hubble Space Telescope [7]. This gage was later used to re-qualify NASA’s rig to assure that the corrective optics would fit when installed during their famous in-orbit repair job.

## Conclusion

At a time when air bearing spindles were being intensely researched by large companies, universities, and government laboratories around the world, it was a little company in Hopkins, Minnesota that ended up making the most accurate spindles in the world. Professional Instruments air bearing spindles have enabled incredibly varied technological advances including glass grinding for high-end cameras, lithographic lenses, and servotrack writing for continually increasing hard disk storage. With our work on Hubble and the Mars Rover, Curiosity, our impact extends beyond planet Earth to Mars. [8]
