## Abstract

Deterministic cutting processes such as diamond turning and diamond grinding are used to efficiently manufacture optical quality surfaces with sub-micrometer form accuracy and nanometer-level surface finish. However, thermal effects continue to be the largest source of dimensional errors and apparent non-repeatability [1]. The costs of being unaware of thermal errors and the root causes of them can be significant. This is evident in the manufacture of optical components which take days to manufacture, but are later found to contain form errors caused by thermal instability [2,3]. One of the major sources of thermal errors is heat generated from the spindle rotation. This paper describes modeling and verification of heat generated due to shearing air films of an air bearing spindle for diamond machining.

## Design

The 10,000 RPM ultra-precision air bearing spindle shown in Figure 1 is produced for diamond turning and grinding applications. Thrust and journal bearings, shown in Figure 2, feature groove compensation with 7-8 µm air films to provide high stiffness. A brushless permanent magnet motor and two encoders are mounted directly to the spindle rotor (frameless). A 1,024 line-count encoder is used for motor commutation and high speed operation, while a 9,000 line-count encoder is used for positioning and low speed operation. Front and rear radial bearings, as well as the motor, are water-cooled.

## Background

“Axial spindle growth” is a term used to describe the displacement of the spindle rotor as a result of thermal issues. A more deterministic approach is to control the heat generated by the spindle. Using Petrov’s equation [4], the heat generated from shearing the air films in hydrostatic journal bearings is accurately predicted assuming the diameter is much larger than the clearance (viz d>100h) and light loading [5].

## Methodology

Bearing radius has the strongest effect on the heat generated from the air films. In addition, as clearance decreases, heat generated increases. This trade-off requires a balance between higher stiffness (larger bearing area and tighter clearance) and reduced heat generation [5]—increasing stiffness results in increased heat generation [6]. To verify the model, deceleration of the rotor caused by the friction torque acting on the rotor is measured. The friction torque is proportionate with the moment of inertia as shown below:

<div class="eq"><math display="block"><mi>T</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>J</mi><mi>α</mi><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mrow><mi>J</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>ω</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>ω</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mrow><msub><mi>t</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>t</mi><mn>1</mn></msub></mrow></mfrac></math></div>

where <i>T</i> is the total friction torque to shear the thrust and journal bearing air films, <i>J</i> is the moment of inertia, and <i>α</i> is the angular acceleration. The angular acceleration is defined as the change in angular velocity <i>ω</i> over time, <i>t</i>.

Solid models of the rotating components are sufficient for calculating the moment of inertia which includes the spindle shaft and test hardware. A relatively simple test can be conducted with a stop watch and a tachometer to estimate the friction torque during spindle coast-down. The spindle is operated at a given speed, then the driving torque is removed and the spindle is allowed to coast to a stop. By measuring the decreasing speed over time, friction torque and power to shear thrust and journal air films can be verified.

During normal spindle operation, heat from the brushless permanent magnet motor must also be considered to adequately size an appropriate chiller [7]. The motor heat is torque dependent but process requirements for diamond turning are typically low.

## Results

Predicted and measured power dissipation due to shear of air bearing films is shown in Figure 3. Results of the coast-down test are in agreement with predicted heat using Petrov’s equation. With 7-8 µm air films and the bearing geometry of the ISO 5.5F spindle, 375 W is generated at 10,000 RPM.

Total heat generation from the motor with 1 Nm of continuous torque (25% of available torque) is shown in Figure 4. Process torque is typically low for many diamond turning applications. Nonetheless, at lower speeds, motor heat is the dominant contributor, whilst at high speed, shearing of the air bearing films dominates. The combined total heat generation at 10,000 RPM with 1 Nm torque is 430 W.

## Conclusion

Petrov’s model is used to calculate the friction heat generated by shearing air films of an ultra-precision air bearing spindle. A simple and effective approach to experimentally verify the model is demonstrated. The total heat generated from friction of the air bearing films and motor losses with 1 Nm torque at 10,000 RPM is 430 W. These results can be used to size an appropriate chiller, and therefore control thermal effects whilst using the spindle for ultra-precision manufacturing processes.
