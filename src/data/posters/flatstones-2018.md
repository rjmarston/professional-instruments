## Abstract

Precision flatstones are an essential tool for use with precision surfaces. When a flatstone is rubbed on such a surface, small asperities and high spots are caught in the porous cavities of the flatstone and are sheared off. The rest of the surface remains largely unchanged. For that reason, flatstones are important for repairing or preparing precision ground surfaces. They remove localized swelling that occurs from surface damage such as nicks and dents. These small swellings from seemingly insignificant incidents can severely inhibit precision assemblies of these parts. The process of removing surface damage with flatstones is also self-limiting; as high spots wear down, material removal decreases until there is full contact of the stone to the surface. This process is demonstrated and improvements are shown for various common examples of surface damage.

## Background

Professional Instruments has been manufacturing and using flatstones to prepare and repair precision ground flat surfaces for over 50 years. The first flatstones were ground by Harold Arneson for his precision assembly work on air bearing spindles. Although the manufacturing process has improved, the original stoning principle still remains the same. Flatstones today are sold commercially and used as a critical component in almost every high precision process employed at Professional Instruments. Furthermore, they have been adopted by seekers of precision across the manufacturing world.

## Flatstones today

Flatstones start as untreated knife sharpening stones, very rough and aggressive and unsuitable for work on a ground workpiece. Made of aluminum oxide (with a Mohs hardness of 9), they have a grit of 150-300, which is unimportant because the gritty surface becomes a porous flat surface (pores ranging from 0.001 to 0.005 inches). Faces and edges are ground to tight flatness and perpendicularity requirements (to permit stoning up to an edge). Another important innovation is packaging the stones as a pair. Before stoning a surface, stones should first be rubbed against each other to ensure that there is no material protruding from the surface of the stone. When the stones are used, they shear off material from the surface being stoned and sometimes that sheared off material can stick out proudly from the stone. Rubbing the stones together cleans them so that the work surface of the stone won’t scratch your workpiece.

## Surface treatment

When a flatstone is rubbed on a surface, small high spots are caught in the porous cavities of the flatstones and are sheared off whilst the undamaged surface remains unaffected. This process is self-limiting because once a flat surface loses its protuberance, the cutting action stops. Bearing down by the user on the stone starts as a relatively high force over a small area forcing high spots into the porous structure of the flatstone to be sheared off by the rubbing action. As defects become less prominent, the applied force becomes distributed across the whole face of the stone. When there are no localized asperities left, cutting action stops and the user is left with two flat surfaces smoothly moving across each other. This stage of treatment is easily discerned as there is a distinct decrease in resistance and difference in the feedback of the stone. The former high spot will also take on a mirror like quality. It is important to remember that this is not a lapping process. When the stone can smoothly slide across the surface, then the surface is ready to be mated or used in a precision assembly. ## Stoning measurement

To demonstrate the effectiveness of flatstones, a part is lightly scratched and then repaired back to functional quality. As shown in Figure 4, edges around the scratch are raised well above the functional plane of the mild steel part. After treatment with flat stones, raised edges of the scratch are sheared and smoother than the rest of the ground surface.

The principle of smoothing high spots is again demonstrated in Figure 5. A profilometer reading is taken on a flat precision ground mild steel plate before and after stoning. Here the small variability in the ground surface is clearly visible and the surface roughness is 8.6 μin. After stoning, the peaks in the surface trace have been sheared off leaving only the low spots under the flat top surface—greatly enhancing the potential bearing area. The surface finish also improved to 6.2 μin.

## Conclusion

Flatstones are essential for repair or preparing precision ground flat surfaces. Damage to a surface often results in raised areas which degrade its functional quality. In the worst case, this can lead to the failure of a precision assembly and the end product, such as an air bearing, will not function. Use of a flatstone is an easy manual process that removes defects and cleans the surface. This is demonstrated and measured on examples of common workplace damage.
